<blockquote class="quote-alt"><p>September 19th, 2019</p><p><strong>Brooklyn</strong></p></blockquote><p>“You’ve gained weight.”&nbsp;</p><p>Visibly, Brooklyn winced.&nbsp;</p><p>Seconds before, she had been halfway through the action of getting into the car. Behind her, her father was putting her backpack into the trunk. Her eyes didn’t lift to find the source of the voice. She didn’t need to look at her to see the sour expression on her face. Brooklyn had memorized it years ago.&nbsp;</p><p>“<em>Aurora</em>,” Caleb hissed. Her father shut the trunk with a thud and turned on her mother.&nbsp;</p><p>“Just stating the obvious,” Aurora mused.&nbsp;</p><p>Brooklyn looked up and locked eyes with her mother. Her eyes, which were so light they hardly counted as blue at all, were staring daggers at her daughter. Her arms were crossed over her chest as she leaned against the hood of the car. Brooklyn knew she’d already done it, but the instant she knew her daughter was watching her, Aurora visibly scanned her body. Then, she clucked her tongue.&nbsp;</p><p>“Hello to you to, Mom.”&nbsp;</p><p>Despite herself, Brooklyn tugged the hem of her t-shirt down as if that would suddenly hide the weight she had put on in her six weeks spent away from her parents. Six weeks that were spent in-patient in Madison, Wisconsin at the Allana Recovery Center, a clinic for those suffering from eating disorders. To this day, Brooklyn still insisted she didn’t belong there. She was neither suffering, nor sick.&nbsp;</p><p>Her mother agreed.&nbsp;</p><p>Her father didn’t.&nbsp;</p><p>“And this is me pointing out the obvious to you to read the goddamn room, Aurora.”&nbsp;</p><p>Her father’s voice was sharp and irritable. Though Brooklyn knew it wasn’t directed at her, she winced. Another argument between them was sure to spark, though there was no way of knowing if it would start here, in the parking lot, or later when they went back to their home: an old school bus renovated to give them anything they needed while her parents traveled the continental United States in search of the ‘next great adventure’.&nbsp;</p><p>As her therapist had pointed out, the damn school bus gave them everything <em>they </em>needed while keeping what <em>Brooklyn </em>needed far out of her reach. She needed stability. Roots. Predictability.</p><p>Instead, she got this.&nbsp;</p><p>“It’s fine, Dad,” Brooklyn murmured as she climbed into the backseat.</p><p>Deescalate. Diffuse. Disappear.</p><p>“It <em>isn’t </em>fine.”</p><p>Brooklyn shut the car door. Outside, she heard snippets of their conversation, though she wished she didn’t. She wished that, for once, they would consider their daughter’s feelings in the matter and do the one thing she never had the guts to ask them to do: shut up.</p><p>“She almost <em>died</em>, Aurora.”</p><p>“She was <em>fine</em>, Caleb. If you’d stop babying her-”</p><p>“Her iron levels were-”</p><p>“Her career-”</p><p>“This isn’t about chasing <em>your </em>demonic dream to-”</p><p>Brooklyn dug into her purse. In seconds, her Airpods were pulled from their case and put into her ears. As she waited for her phone to power on, she tried not to focus on the conversation outside, which was quickly becoming heated. Each sentence rose in volume. Brooklyn hit play on the first song she could pull up and slid the bar to crank it to full volume. AC/DC blared in her ears.&nbsp;</p><p><em>I’m on the highway to hell…</em></p><p>Fitting.&nbsp;</p><p>Brooklyn tilted her head back against the seat and closed her eyes. The song didn’t matter. She just didn’t want to hear her parents fall into <em>another </em>argument in which she was unwillingly forced into the starring role. Over the years, they made sure she learned her place: as the one who had interrupted their bliss. She was an unwanted child. A mistake. A pinprick in a condom. A forgotten pill.&nbsp;</p><p>They never wanted her.&nbsp;</p><p>Every day, they proved it in one way or another.&nbsp;</p><p>Her mother’s snide comments were a constant, just as her father ignored her as often as possible. Despite the appearance of concern now, Brooklyn knew it was a show. The only reason either of them agreed to the facility was because the social workers gave them little choice in the matter. When they returned to the bus, hooked up the car to the trailer, and pulled back on the road, she would become invisible once more.&nbsp;</p><p>Just as she liked it.&nbsp;</p><p>“Brooklyn I am talking to-”&nbsp;</p><p>The music stopped abruptly as the left earbud was ripped from her ear. Her eyes flew open as she took in the sight of her mother leaning into the backseat. Brooklyn hadn’t realized they’d gotten into the car, or that they were flying down the highway now. Her father was gripping the steering wheel so tightly that his knuckles had turned white. He lost the argument. She would be smug.&nbsp;</p><p>“Sorry,” Brooklyn pulled the other earbud from her ear and tucked it into the case. The other one was plucked from her mother’s fingers and received the same treatment. She put her phone and headphones into her purse as slowly as she could. Anything to keep from looking at her. “What were you saying?”&nbsp;</p><p>“How much did you gain?”&nbsp;</p><p>“I don’t know.”&nbsp;</p><p><em>Liar. You do know. You gained 15lbs. You hate it as much as she does.&nbsp;</em></p><p>“It’s all in your face.”</p><p><em>I</em> <em>know. I’m well aware.</em></p><p>“I didn’t have much of a choice, Mom.”</p><p>Brooklyn knew where this was going. It was where she had already been multiple times over the last few weeks. With every ounce she put on, she hated herself more and more. She had no choice. With doctors and nurses hovering over her and counting every calorie she put into her body, Brooklyn had no power over the situation.&nbsp;</p><p>“Now you do.”&nbsp;</p><p>“I know.”&nbsp;</p><p>“I made tonics for you.”&nbsp;</p><p>“Thanks.”&nbsp;</p><p>Brooklyn thought of the bitter tonics her mother had introduced her to just under three years ago. Infused with magic, each one provided the Witch enough to keep her body alive, but without the calorie content of the cheeseburgers she used to crave or the carbs of the endless breadsticks they advertised on television.&nbsp;</p><p>The tonics were meant for illnesses. They were meant for short terms in which a Witch or Warlock might be without food, like camping or long hikes. They were meant for emergencies. They weren’t, unfortunately, meant to replace food entirely.&nbsp;</p><p>And yet…</p><p>“I also got you a multivitamin,” Aurora stated. Her eyes were kind now. She was getting what she wanted. Brooklyn smiled in return. It didn’t meet her eyes, but it provided her mother the incentive to keep going. Her daughter was listening. “It will help keep your iron up. So you don’t pass out again.”&nbsp;</p><p>Fainting.&nbsp;</p><p>It was the reason this all started.&nbsp;</p><p>Six weeks ago, when she woke up in the emergency room, she hadn’t remembered fainting at all. What she <em>did </em>remember was going for a run. The early August heat had been unbearable, but she ran every morning and wouldn’t stop now. Brooklyn didn’t remember collapsing or the man who had meant well by calling 911. She just remembered waking up in the emergency room with a crowd of doctors, nurses, and social workers saying words she hated to hear.&nbsp;</p><p><em>Anorexia.</em></p><p><em>Impossibly low iron.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>Eating disorder.</em></p><p><em>Gaining weight.</em></p><p><em>Dieting.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>Running.&nbsp;</em></p><p>Brooklyn remembered the way her mother clutched at her father’s shirt as she feigned being upset into his chest. Tears were streaming down her face, though they weren’t her real ones. They were actress tears. Her eyes weren’t even red. She pretended, just as he did, to be concerned. She faked innocence and ignorance to the things her daughter did. She acted her way out of responsibility yet again.&nbsp;</p><p>When they checked her into the facility, the doctors explained to her parents that visiting was encouraged. Dr. Edwards sat in her office with the brochures for them that detailed the importance of family involvement in times like this. Brooklyn remembered smiling at her clueless doctor as she explained the studies. Dr. Edwards, though sweet, had no idea what she was dealing with.&nbsp;</p><p>Three days later, with no visits or phone calls, Dr. Edwards had requested Brooklyn to stay after one of their sessions. Brooklyn remembered the sad, sad look in her eyes as she apologized for the lack of participation from her parents.&nbsp;</p><p><em>“What state are they in this time?” Brooklyn asked, crossing her arms over her chest. Her voice was devoid of emotion. She expected this.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“Brooklyn-”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“Is it another country? That would be new, since Mom hates planes, but not surprising.”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“New Mexico.”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>Dr. Edwards pulled her glasses off and rubbed at her face. She looked tired. Brooklyn wondered if this was the first time she’d experienced this. She wondered, too, if this was the job Dr. Edwards wanted when she went into medical school. Did she grow up dreaming of days she would spend convincing sad, broken little girls that their worth wasn’t on a scale? Or, the more likely scenario, had she dreamed of surgeries and specialties, but gotten terrible grades in medical school?&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“And we’re in Wyomin-”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“Wisconsin,” She corrected.</em></p><p><em>“Wisconsin,” Brooklyn replied as if she were commenting on something mundane. To her it was. The treatment was just as important to her as the weather outside or the color of the nurse’s scrubs. “Listen, Dr. Edwards, you’re not going to get them involved. Unless it involves what they find important, you’re not going to get what you’re looking for.”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“Are you implying that-”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>Dr. Edwards cut herself off. Brooklyn smiled. She knew where that sentence was going. Her doctor might not have been able to finish it, but Brooklyn could.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“That I’m not important to them? That was made abundently clear years ago.”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“I’m sure your-”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“My parents dumped me in St. Louis on my grandparents when I was three days old. Dad’s parents were already gone, and then Mom’s parents passed away. Grandpa first, when I was two, and Grandma when I was five. Children’s Division threatened them with abandonment charges, so they brought me on the road with them.”</em></p><p><em>“I see.”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“My father is too busy shooting his documentaries and editing them on his computer to remember he has a daughter. My mother is obsessed with my ‘modeling career’, which is just the ones her parents wouldn’t let her have. When we aren’t shooting documentaries, we’re traveling across the country to agents and shoots looking for my ‘big break’.”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“And that’s when your mother pays attention to you?”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“No,” Brooklyn stated with a shake of her head. “That’s when my mother pays attention to the ghost of her teenage dreams. I just happen to be the vessel that carries that burden.”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“Oh,” Dr. Edwards murmured.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“Still think they care?” Brooklyn tilted her head, her blue eyes laser focused on her doctor’s.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>Dr. Edwards rubbed her forehead again. She did that a lot, Brooklyn would come to realize, when she needed a moment to collect her thoughts. She did it when the situation was too fucked up for words. In Brooklyn’s case, she saw it often. Finally, she put her glasses back on her face and studied her.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>Brooklyn wondered what she saw in her. Most of the other fifteen year old girls in the treatment center spoke of their boyfriends or the friend groups they would be returning to. They spoke of Homecoming and cheerleading. They dreamed of popularity and trips to the mall. They gushed about it. Brooklyn, on the other hand, had never stayed in one city for more than two nights. Well, except for now, in the one place none of them were looking to make a friend. She did her schooling through the Internet, entirely independently. Unlike the other girls, Brooklyn had no one, not even the people who brought her into this world and were supposed to love her.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>“Well, Brooklyn,” Dr. Edwards mused as she leaned forward to rest her elbows on the desk. “If they won’t care, then you and I will. And we have work to do.”</em>&nbsp;</p><p>“Did you hear me?”&nbsp;</p><p>Her mother’s voice pulled her from her thoughts. Brooklyn glanced up and shook her head. She hadn’t heard. She hadn’t been paying attention at all. The car was parked at the campsite now. Her father had already climbed out of the car to go inside the bus. Her mother was half hanging in the backseat to watch her.&nbsp;</p><p>“No, I’m sorry.”&nbsp;</p><p>“I <em>said </em>that this little setback isn’t the end of your career, Brookie,” Aurora stated in her sweet, singsong voice. She reached back to rub her arm. Brooklyn somehow managed not to cringe away from her touch. “You’ll lose the weight. You’ll be beautiful again. And then we’ll have everything we’ve ever wanted.”&nbsp;</p><p>“I know,” Brooklyn smiled, reaching up to tuck a lock of hair behind her mother’s ear. “Can we go inside and get one of the tonics? This was snack time, and I’m starting to feel hungry.”&nbsp;</p><p>“That’s my girl,” Aurora nodded. She leaned forward to brush a stray lock of Brooklyn’s hair from her face. “Let’s go inside and have a tonic together, shall we? Then we’ll do a conditioning treatment on your hair. It’s not shiny, and no one will think you’re beautiful if your hair isn’t shiny.”&nbsp;</p><p>“Of course. Anything for you, Mom.”&nbsp;</p><p><em>You were wrong, Dr. Edwards, </em>Brooklyn thought as she studied her mother. <em>You might have cared, but I don’t. I don’t have my own dreams, so hers will do, won’t they?&nbsp;</em></p>
